Whose logbooks?

CLIWOC was a European Union research project (2001–2003) of climate scientists and historians from Spain, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands and Argentina. They chose and transcribed over 1,700 logbooks from the archives above, mainly for 1750–1850, and turned the officers' wind words into numbers. Version 2.1 is published by DANS, the Dutch national data archive.

The balance reflects which archives the team could reach and fund, not how many ships sailed. Dutch logbooks are about 45% of the entries, and after 1830 almost all of them, from the collection of the Dutch weather institute KNMI. British logbooks are about 35%, mostly from the National Maritime Museum and the East India Company's records at the British Library. Spain is 17%; France, then a major naval power, barely 2%; Portuguese, Danish and other fleets are almost absent. So where the map looks busy, it may show which logbooks survived and were chosen, rather than where most ships actually sailed.

How a ship knew where it was

Out of sight of land, an officer worked like someone crossing a dark field with eyes closed: he knew where he had started, which way he was facing, and how fast he was going. Adding these up hour by hour gave the reckoned position. When the sky allowed, the sun at noon (or the stars) gave the true latitude, and later in the period a chronometer could give longitude: the observed position. The difference was the push of wind and current that nobody aboard could feel.

CLIWOC records which it was. About 36% of latitudes and 40% of longitudes on this map are reckoned only. On about 54,000 days a logbook gives both: the reckoned latitude was off by a median of 11 km, a little more as the wind rose; the reckoned longitude by a median of 90 km. (These double entries are almost all Dutch.)

On the map, tracks are solid where the position was observed and dashed where it was reckoned. In a ship's whole-voyage view, a small circle marks where the officer thought the ship was, joined to where the sky showed it to be.

Each ship's noon positions over the storm and the days around it. Each feathered arrow is that day's logged wind, pointing the way it blew, with one feather per two Beaufort forces; storm days in ochre. Ships on different sides of a storm log different winds: together they outline it. The ochre spiral is a sketch of the storm's form: by Buys Ballot's rule, with your back to the wind the storm's centre lies to your left (right in the southern hemisphere), nearer when the wind is stronger.
