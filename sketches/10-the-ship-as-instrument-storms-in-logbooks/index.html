The Ship as Instrument: Storms in Logbooks

What does a storm look like when the instrument is a ship, and what changes when the sailors' words become data?

10 · Christos Montsenigos · started 2026-11-09

Event

Before there were reliable wind gauges, officers at sea judged the wind themselves. They watched how fast the ship went and how much sail it could safely carry, and wrote what they saw in the logbook in words: a moderate breeze, a topsail breeze, a storm. Every day at noon they also noted the ship's position. The researchers who digitised these logs call this "semi-instrumental": the sails were the instrument.

Thousands of these logbooks survive. The CLIWOC project typed in about 280,000 noon entries from Dutch, British, Spanish and French ships between 1662 and 1855, and translated their wind words into Beaufort numbers, the scale used today. This sketch looks at storms, where the instrument is pushed hardest, and at what happens to the sailors' words on the way to becoming a number.

Interpretive: my framing, drawing on the sources listed below.

A first look: 7 March 1796

In early March 1796 a group of Dutch ships left Texel and sailed north around Scotland. On 7 March six of them, all near 63°N, logged a storm. Each ship wrote it down in its own words, and the database turns those words into two different wind speeds.

The same storm, met by ships up to about 200 km apart, becomes 22.6 or 26.8 m/s depending on whether an officer wrote "stormy weather" or "storm". In the logbook data, the ships heading for the Cape of Good Hope all end their logs at Saldanha Bay, north of Cape Town, between 6 and 17 August 1796.

What this sketch cannot show yet: the wind speeds are estimates for words, not measurements; positions were often worked out by dead reckoning, not observed; the logs give one noon entry a day, so we don't know what happened in between; and a ship that wrote nothing was not necessarily in calm weather. What happened at Saldanha Bay in 1796 still needs a historical source.
