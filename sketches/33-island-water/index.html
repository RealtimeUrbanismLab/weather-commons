Island Water

Where does an Aegean island's water come from, who decides, and what would a fairer and less fragile water future look like?

About 87% of the islands' rain falls between October and March, and in the winter of 2022–23 all 105 islands in this sketch got less than their 1991–2020 average reanalysed. Desalination capacity has grown fast: of the 94,400 m³ a day listed on the islands in 2020, 3,200 m³ had come online by 1990 documented. In 2024–26 water emergencies were declared or reported on 12 of the 105 islands documented reported. The sketch has three parts. Freshwater map sets the islands side by side: rain each winter since 1991, desalination per resident, emergencies, and the rain that lands on every roof. Cisterns fills a rooftop cistern on Sifnos with the rain of each day from 1991 to 2026. The Sifnos case follows the island's 2024 emergency, and Poulati, a valley where nine springs are shared in twelve-hour turns written into contracts.

Freshwater map

In October–March 2022–23, 100 of the 105 islands received less than 80% of their 1991–2020 average rain, and 28 had a drier winter than Sifnos, relative to their own average. In 2023–24, 104 of the 105 were below average reanalysed.

In 2024 the General Secretariat for Civil Protection declared water emergencies in 33 municipalities across Greece, on eight of the sketch's islands: Serifos (30 April), Spetses (4 June), Poros, Sifnos (both 20 June), Leros (25 June), eight municipalities of Crete (20 June to 9 September), northern Evia (2 October) and Andros (12 November). Each was declared for one month, for the consequences that "may arise" from water shortage. On Spetses, Poros and Sifnos they ended within two months; on Andros and on four of the Cretan municipalities they were extended month by month to 31 December 2025, and on Minoa Pediadas to 14 January 2026. In 2025 Poros was declared again, and Lesvos (West Lesvos and Mytilene) and Chios were added documented. Leros and Patmos were declared in drought by ministerial decision in November 2025, and Astypalaia was reported in a water emergency in May 2026 reported.

    Every roof a cistern?

    Footprints traced from aerial imagery show 7,113 buildings on Sifnos with 59 ha of roof; the median roof is 55 m² cartographic. In an average water year about 175,000 m³ of rain lands on them, and about 127,000 m³ in the dry year 2022–23 reanalysed calculated. That is 115% and 83% of what the island's 2,777 residents would use in a year at 150 litres a day each synthetic, or 78 and 56 days of its desalination capacity. It is an upper bound: it counts every roof, drained to a tank large enough to hold a winter's rain. Most of the rain falls between October and March; the Cisterns tab shows how much one house can keep into summer.

    The same count on the other islands finds 613,641 buildings on 85 islands, with 6,838 ha of roof cartographic. Of the 37 islands with 1,000 residents or more, 20 get more rain on their roofs in an average year than their residents would use at 150 litres a day; in each island's driest year since 1991–92, 1 does calculated. The driest year was 1999–2000 or 2000–01 on 23 of them.

    Rain on roofs = roof area × 0.8 runoff × ERA5 rain for the water year (October–September), one ERA5 cell per island; ERA5 is about 14% wetter than stations on Chios, so these may be high. Residents' use = 2021 residents × 150 litres × 365 days. Summer visitors are not counted, and the roofs include hotels, holiday homes, churches and sheds, so islands with many visitors score high here. Footprints: Microsoft Global ML Building Footprints (traced by machine learning from Bing imagery; ODbL), 7,113 on Sifnos against 1,916 in OpenStreetMap; some sheds, ruins and shadows may be counted and some roofs missed.     Desalination over time

    The earliest island plant in the records is a 1,000 m³-a-day multi-stage flash (thermal) plant on Syros, online in 1970 and presumed offline by 2020. Of the plants still listed in 2020, those online by 1990 add up to 3,200 m³ a day, and all of them to 94,400 m³ a day documented. Since 2024 the state has granted temporary permits to install and run portable units under a fast-track procedure: 29 decisions, 16,550 m³ a day documented.

    The Sifnos case

    In June 2024 a desalination unit broke down at Platys Gialos, on the south coast of Sifnos. Some settlements went without tap water for ten to fifteen days, and at some points on the island June use was up to twice the previous August's reported. On 20 June the General Secretariat for Civil Protection declared a water emergency on Sifnos. Serifos, the next island to the north-west, had been under one since 30 April documented.

    Where the water is

    Sifnos covers 74 km² and rises to 675 m. Its 2,777 residents get water from groundwater, springs, private rooftop cisterns, a storage reservoir reported and three desalination units: two seawater units at Platys Gialos (500 and 1,000 m³ a day) and one at Kamares that treats brackish water (750 m³ a day) documented. About 80% of the island's electricity comes from an oil-fired power station reported. The blue lines show where water would run off the terrain; the thick one is the valley of Poulati.

    Terrain: Copernicus DEM GLO-30, 50 m contours (cartographic). Blue lines: flow paths computed from the terrain, not mapped streams (calculated). Villages and the monastery: OpenStreetMap. Squares: desalination units, drawn at the village they serve; their exact sites are not mapped.

    The winters before

    The springs, wells and cisterns of Sifnos are refilled by rain that falls between October and March. In 2022–23 that rain was 66% of the 1991–2020 average for the island; in 2023–24, the winter before the breakdown, 74%. Winters 2022–23 to 2024–25 are drawn in full colour.

    Poulati: nine springs, fourteen waters

    From the edge of Artemonas, the valley of Poulati runs about 900 m east down to the sea below the monastery of Panagia Poulati (built 1865–1872) cartographic. Nine springs rise along it, from 140 m to sea level. Their water is collected in stone tanks set one below another beside the riverbed: 21 open-air cisterns (χαβούζες) of 15–20 m³ and about ten troughs (γούρνες) of 3 m³. Five of the cisterns, near springs 1, 3, 5, 6 and 7, are communal: they take water straight from a spring and pass it on to secondary cisterns lower down, through stone furrows (κουτέντο) now mostly replaced by plastic pipe reported. The cisterns were left uncovered, so everyone could see how much water was stored reported.

    Profile: Copernicus DEM GLO-30 along the valley line (cartographic, vertical scale exaggerated). Springs and cisterns: from Zepou & Matsaganis (2024). The chapter gives the order of the springs and their range, 0–140 m, not each one's height or position, so they are spaced evenly here (schematic).

    The week, in waters

    Water is shared by time. A plot opens the outlet to its own cistern for an agreed number of twelve-hour spans, called "waters", on set days of the week. The right belongs to the land, not the owner, and since the early twentieth century it has been written into sale and inheritance contracts. Overflows and drain-offs (στραγγίδια) are shared by unwritten agreement: "no water is ever lost". In a drought, farmed plots are watered first reported.

The threats the farmers name are building, drilling into the same aquifer as the springs, and the 2015 cadastre, which put the main old communal cistern on private land, separated from the spring that feeds it reported.

    In the same summers, water for the main settlements was allocated by municipal restrictions, tanker deliveries and an emergency decision of Civil Protection; in Poulati it was allocated by the weekday turns recorded in deeds interpretive.
